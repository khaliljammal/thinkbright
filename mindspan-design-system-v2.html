Design system v2 — replaces the dark version

Clear ground.
Colour means something.

The app lives on cool porcelain — not black, not stark white. Cards sit on it as pure white, so depth comes from the tint rather than shadows and gradients. Then the whole thing inverts the moment a timed task begins.

What I cut, and why

  • The near-black ground. You called it right — dark background plus one bright accent is the most common AI-design default going. It also fought the audience: 35–65 year olds reading small numbers do better on light.
  • Coloured primary buttons. Now the primary action is ink. Colour is reserved entirely for the six domains, so any colour on screen carries meaning.
  • The third typeface. Two families, not three. Fewer moving parts reads cleaner.
  • Uppercase mono labels on every section. That tic was doing most of the work in making it feel machine-made. Mono now appears only where there are actual numbers.
  • Numbered section markers. The sections aren't a sequence, so numbering them was decoration pretending to be structure.

The ground

Porcelain has a faint blue-violet cast. It's the difference between a surface that looks considered and one that looks like an unstyled default — and it stops the page reading as cream, which is its own overused look.

Ink is deep indigo rather than black. On a light ground pure black looks like a printing error; a blue-shifted near-black looks intentional and is easier to read for longer.

The rule that keeps it clean

Colour is only ever a domain. Buttons are ink, states are ink, chrome is ink. So when something on screen is violet, it means working memory — every time, with no exceptions to remember.

Why amber isn't the brand colour any more

Amber can't hold contrast on a light ground without turning brown. Rather than compromise it, amber moves to where it's genuinely brilliant: the stimulus colour in focus mode, at nearly 10:1 against dark.

The signature: the room dims

The app is calm and light all day. Tap Start and the screen inverts to deep indigo, everything but the task disappears, and the stimulus glows amber. It's memorable — and unlike most signature moments, it's doing real work.

Focus mode

Dark ground gives the stimulus maximum contrast, which the measurement depends on. It removes every competing element from view. And it tells the user, without a word of copy, that this part counts.

Circle to respond, square to withhold.

Shape carries the meaning alongside colour, so the task stays valid for colour-blind users.

Two typefaces

Instrument Sans for everything a person reads. IBM Plex Mono only where there are literal numbers — units, timers, milliseconds. When mono shows up, it means data.

Keeping it clean

Most of what makes an interface feel machine-made is additive. These are the things to keep leaving out.

Do

  • Let white cards float on tinted ground — depth without shadow
  • Keep one primary action per screen, in ink
  • Use colour only to mean a domain
  • Set every number in tabular figures
  • Leave more space than feels necessary
  • Save the full-screen inversion for tasks that are being measured

Don't

  • Gradients on cards, or glows outside focus mode
  • Uppercase mono labels as decoration
  • Numbered markers where there's no real sequence
  • Drop shadows to fake hierarchy the tint already gives you
  • Mascots, confetti, badges, gems, streak guilt
  • A third typeface, however tempting
